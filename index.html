Observability Limits of Monocular Shuttlecock 3D Reconstruction from Broadcast Badminton Video

Gino Chang

Project page · 2026

Abstract

Recovering the 3D flight of a shuttlecock from a single broadcast camera is attractive and, for badminton, unsolved: no public 3D ground truth exists, and every published method validates on real footage with 2D reprojection error alone. We show that this practice cannot distinguish a correct reconstruction from a wrong one. On synthetic strokes with known 3D, solutions whose 2D reprojection differs by only 0.07–0.20 px differ in true 3D error by up to 46× for smashes.

We derive a per-stroke observability number N — the departure of the image track from anything an unaccelerated 3D motion could produce, in units of tracker noise — that needs no 3D truth and predicts the Cramér–Rao bound on 3D error (CRLB ∝ N−0.98, R² = 0.83, partial ρ = −0.59 after controlling for track length). A learned 2D→3D lifter trained on 78,702 synthetic strokes anchored to real annotations and rendered through ten cameras calibrated from real broadcasts reaches 0.24 m median error on held-out synthetic data — yet beats the unbiased bound by up to 5.5× exactly where N is lowest, i.e. where the answer comes from the prior rather than the image. On real TrackNetV3-tracked rallies its error rises to 0.87 m and physical validity falls from 98.9% to 79.9%; per stroke type, that validity is predicted by N (Spearman +0.83) but not by the synthetic test error (−0.31).

1. The evaluation problem

A point object seen from one camera lies on a ray; nothing in a single frame fixes where along it. What makes a shuttle trajectory recoverable at all is that gravity and air drag bend the flight over time, and the bend leaves a signature in the image. When the observed arc is short or nearly aligned with the viewing direction, that signature is small — and reprojection error stops saying anything about depth.

This matters because the field's current state of the art (MonoTrack, CVPR-W 2022; SynthNet, MMSports 2024; Uplifting Table Tennis, WACV 2026) validates real-footage 3D with exactly this metric, and states explicitly that no 3D ground truth exists for broadcast video.

2. Method

2.1 Calibrating the broadcast camera from the court and the net

A ground-plane homography cannot recover the intrinsics — on this data the two Zhang constraints disagree by 65%. It pins down everything on the floor and nothing above it, and the missing degree of freedom is exactly the vertical one a trajectory is made of. The net supplies it: a structure of known height (1.55 m at the posts, sagging to 1.524 m) at a known court position, spanning the image as a near-straight line that can be located to sub-pixel precision.

We locate the painted court lines (sub-pixel ridge, 167–312 samples per image) and the net cord (270 samples, 0.14 px fit residual), then fit a rigid pinhole camera to court + net in a right-handed frame. The court plane alone would fit any camera exactly (a plane-to-image map is an 8-DoF homography); the off-plane net is what makes the calibration determined.

2.2 Synthetic strokes anchored to real annotations

Every training stroke is tied to one real annotated stroke: its start and end court positions and its flight time come from the ShuttleSet-format labels (13,129 strokes, 17 matches); only the height profile — the one quantity never observed — is synthesised, by solving a boundary-value problem under quadratic drag (terminal velocity 6.8 m/s). 99.8% of 24,000 solves converge, and 98.9% of the resulting trajectories clear the net although the solver never sees it. Strokes are then projected through the ten measured cameras with 2 px tracker noise and 5% dropout: 78,702 samples, split by anchor so no real stroke appears on both sides.

2.3 Lifter

A 2.7M-parameter transformer receives, per frame, the world-space viewing ray through the detection, the ray's floor intersection, a near-horizontal flag and time, plus the camera centre; it predicts relative depth along each ray and the 3D point is recovered geometrically. Calibration therefore lives in the geometry rather than being relearned. Depth, not height, is the output: 1.9% of frames look up at the shuttle from these 5–13 m cameras, and a height parameterisation diverges there.

Synthetic pipelines usually sample camera parameters from assumed ranges. Calibrating real broadcasts instead gives a measured distribution — and it does not overlap the ranges assumed for the closest published pipeline.

Three quality gates select the ten: net-cord residual <1 px, sag within 10 mm of specification, and far-baseline depth resolution ≥12 px/m. The sag gate catches failures the fit residual misses — three venues fitted the cord to 0.2–0.4 px yet implied sags of 9, −15 and −49 mm. A fit residual proves you fitted something; only an independent physical invariant proves you fitted the right thing.

4. An image-computable observability number

A constant-velocity 3D motion projects to a straight image line whose arc-length runs as a Möbius function of time. Fitting that model to the observed track and taking the residual — in units of the 2D noise — isolates exactly the signature gravity and drag leave in the image. We call it N. It uses no 3D information, so it can be computed on real footage before reconstructing anything.

5. A learned lifter beats the bound — where the image says least

The lifter reaches 0.364 m mean / 0.244 m median 3D error on held-out synthetic strokes (baselines: constant depth 2.04 m; assume-on-the-floor 9.66 m). Compared per stroke type against the Cramér–Rao bound computed on the same strokes, it sits honestly above the bound where N is high, and below it where N is low.

6. Real footage

Real rallies were tracked with TrackNetV3 (best checkpoint, 0.940 validation accuracy; detection 9–92% per venue) and lifted with the same model through the same calibrated cameras. There is no 3D truth, so the reconstruction is scored against what the annotation and physics do know: every stroke must clear the net, and both ends of every stroke carry an annotated court position — a real horizontal anchor the model never sees.

Calibration is not the limiting term. At the endpoint the depth ambiguity vanishes (the shuttle is on a known plane), so calibration becomes the leading camera-side error — and it still inflates the endpoint error by only 3–7%. In pixel-equivalent terms the reconstruction error (23.6 px median) is fourteen times the calibration residual (1.7 px).

7. Comparison with MonoTrack-style physics fitting

MonoTrack reconstructs each shot by fitting launch position, velocity and a per-shot drag coefficient under forward-Euler quadratic drag, minimising an L6 reprojection loss with SLSQP. We re-implemented that step from its public code and ran it on the same 2D tracks and the same calibrated cameras as the lifter — the comparison the paper needs is the 3D-from-2D step on identical input. Deviations from the original (no hit detector, so each annotated stroke is one shot; the receiving-player drift term is dropped) are documented in the baseline's source.

9. Limitations

Real-footage validation rests on proxies: net clearance and annotated court positions at stroke ends. Rally-final floor landings — the one true 3D anchor — were unusable because the tracker loses the shuttle as it lands (0 of 10 candidates with ≥5 detections); tracking more of the 1,936 available rallies is the direct fix. Tracker coverage limited the real set to 177 of 9,875 candidate strokes. The self-calibration residual sits at 1.65 px, decomposed as 1.06 px of court-detection inconsistency plus 0.61 px from reconciling the net; lens distortion, line placement and camera drift were each tested and ruled out (lines straight to 0.12 px, drift 0.04 px, line offsets 1.2 mm median). The net tape sits a measured 15 mm below the nominal cord height, consistently across venues.
